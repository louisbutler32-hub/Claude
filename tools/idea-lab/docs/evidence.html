Does title wording predict views?

Before building a tool that scores YouTube titles, I tested whether title wording predicts anything at all. It doesn't. Here is the test, why one result looked like a finding, and what survived.

Across 677 real videos in 19 niches, no measurable relationship between a title's surface form and how the video performed relative to its channel.

The test

Four ways of asking, one honest answer

Each bar is a model trying to pick which videos beat their own channel's baseline, using only the title. The scale is AUC: 0.500 is a coin flip. Bars run left or right of that line.

The shaded band is the noise floor, measured rather than assumed — labels were shuffled and every model refitted 500 times to see how well it scores on data with no signal in it at all. A bar inside the band is indistinguishable from chance.

The trap

Why the red bar is not a finding

The 0.590 result is the one a person would ship. It clears the noise floor and a permutation test puts it at p = 0.004. Stop there and you have a confident-looking "title score".

It is an artefact of how the data was split. Random folds put videos from the same niche in both training and test sets, so the model can learn which niche a video belongs to and ride that niche's baseline — nothing to do with title craft.

The tell is in the weights. These are the words the model leaned on hardest:

film · power · dragon · football · house · money · transformation

Those are subject-matter labels, not persuasion devices. Dragon identifies Dragon Ball videos; football identifies football videos. Split the data so the model has to predict a niche it has never seen, and the score falls to 0.499 — chance, to three decimal places.

Training and testing entirely inside one niche is worse still: a mean of 0.467 across twelve niches, with 4 of 12 above chance where a fair coin gives 6.

The baseline model is the genuinely predictive part, and it is cross-validated, not fitted-and-quoted. It answers the question that actually matters when someone sets a goal: what view count is normal for a channel this size, in this niche? Everything about goal feasibility rests on it.

The CTR figure is derived, not guessed

"Half of all channels and videos on YouTube have an impressions CTR that can range between 2% and 10%."

support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154 — YouTube's own documentation

"Half of all" is an interquartile range, and an interquartile range pins a log-normal distribution exactly: median 4.47%. The derivation round-trips to 2.00% and 10.00%, so it reproduces the published figures rather than approximating them. Every third-party "good CTR" number I found instead cited another blog post.

Nobody can read a competitor's CTR

YouTube exposes impressions and click-through rate only to a video's own owner, inside Studio. There is no public or partner API for it. Any tool showing you a rival's CTR is inferring or inventing it — so every CTR number here is labelled MODELLED, and your own real figure from Studio can replace the prior.

Consequences

What to change on Monday

Stop spending time here

A/B-ing ALL CAPS, emoji, numbers, superlatives and bracket tags. Across 677 videos in 19 niches these carry no measurable effect on how a video performs relative to its channel. Individually, not one of them cleared the noise floor.

Spend it here instead

Topic choice and impression volume. When a goal is out of reach it is almost never the title — it is that the goal needs several times the impressions a typical video in the niche receives, which is a distribution problem.

This is a narrow claim, and worth stating precisely: it says surface form does not predict performance relative to channel size. It does not say titles are irrelevant — a title still has to represent the video honestly and make its subject legible. It says the packaging tricks are not the lever.

The first three figures belong to videos performing comparably: 4.04M views on 462K subscribers scored 983.98, while 3.81M on 334K scored 5687.77. A label that behaves like that cannot be a target variable, so the corpus computes its own transparent one instead.

The kept format data gets the same scepticism. avg_outlier is an arithmetic mean of a heavy-tailed quantity, so "How [thing] Is Made" reports 130.3× off 169 uses. Shrunk toward the corpus median with weight uses / (uses + 150), that becomes 46.2×, while a 1,419-use format barely moves.

Reproduce it

Run the test yourself

node scripts/build-model.mjs   # refits and re-runs leave-one-niche-out
node scripts/power-test.mjs    # 500-permutation null distribution
node scripts/within-niche.mjs  # within-niche predictive test
npm test                       # 39 tests

The corpus ships with the tool: 729 real videos with provenance, collection date, and the exclusion rules applied — under 14 days old, under 100 views, or auto-generated channels, the first two following YouTube's own warning that such figures are unstable.

A test in the suite asserts the null result still holds. If it ever starts passing, that is a signal to check for leakage before claiming predictive power — which is exactly the trap the red bar sets.
